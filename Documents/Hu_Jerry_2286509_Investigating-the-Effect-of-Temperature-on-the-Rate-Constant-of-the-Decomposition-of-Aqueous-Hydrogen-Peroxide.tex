\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
\indent \indent The decomposition of hydrogen peroxide (\ce{H2O2}) to form oxygen and water is a thermodynamically favorable process, but unless catalyzed, it proceeds at an extremely slow rate at room temperature.

\begin{center}
  \ce{2H2O2_{(aq)} -> 2H2O_{(l)} + O2_{(g)}}
\end{center}

\noindent In this investigation, potassium iodide (\ce{KI}) was used as a catalyst so that the reaction (specifically, the formation of oxygen gas) inside the system can occur at a measurable rate within a practical time frame. This investigation aims to explore how temperature impacts the rate constant for the catalyzed decomposition reaction of aqeuous hydrogen peroxide. The rate of the reaction is observed indirectly by measuring the initial rate of increase in gas pressure inside a sealed flask, as oxygen gas (\ce{O2_{(g)}}) builds up.

\begin{equation}
  \ce{2H2O2_{(aq)} ->[\ce{KI}] 2H2O_{(l)} + O2_{(g)}}
\end{equation}

% \subsection{Background Concepts}
% \label{sec:introduction-background-concepts}
The collision theory states that a chemical reaction can only occur when particles collide with sufficient energy and the correct orientation. As the temperature of the reaction increase, reacting particles have greater average kinetic energy. This increase in the particle's collision ferquency and the increase in the probability of collisions with energy \textit{equal or greater than} the activation energy ($\text{E}_\text{A}$). Thus, increasing the temperature of the reaction is expected to increase the rate of the reaction and the reaction constant. The temperature dependence of a rate constant can be modelled by the Arrhenius equation:

\begin{equation}
  k = A \cdot e^{- \frac{E_{A}}{RT}}
\label{eq:arrhenius-equation}
\end{equation}

\noindent Where $k$ is the rate constant, $A$ is the frequency factor, $E_{A}$ is the activation energy, $R$ is the gas constant ($8.31\ \text{J}\ \text{K}^{-1}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$)\footnote{Value obtained from IB Chemistry Data Booklet, version 1.0 (first assessment 2025).}, and $T$ is the temperature, in Kelvins. Taking the natural logarithm on both sides of the equation will yield a linear relationship:

\begin{equation}
  \ln{(K)} = - \frac{E_{A}}{RT} + \ln{(A)}
\end{equation}

\noindent Where the slope of the linear graph is $- \frac{E_{A}}{R}$, and the y-intercept is $\ln{A}$. Because this experiment uses pressure measurements rather than concentration measurements, the ideal gas law can be used to relate pressure with the amount of oxygen produced:

% As oxygen gas (\ce{O2_{(g)}}) forms in the sealed flask of approximate constant gas volume, the pressure increases. Assuming ideal gas behaviour, the pressure should increase as $n$ (the number of moles of gas) increases, but $V$ (volume) stays constant:

\begin{equation}
  PV = nRT
\end{equation}

\noindent In a sealed flask, the gas volume is considered as constant. The equation is re-rranged to show that moles of gas depends on both pressure and temperature:

\begin{equation}
  n = \frac{PV}{RT}
\end{equation}

\noindent Then, for a given $\Delta{P}$, the change in moles is:

\begin{equation}
  \Delta{n(\ce{O2})} = \frac{V_\text{gas}}{RT} \cdot \Delta{P}
\label{eq:oxygen-production-relationship}
\end{equation}

% \noindent Given that the initial concentration of \ce{H2O2} and the amount of catalyst added were held constant across all trials, the calculated rate constant can be compared across temperature levels.

\noindent Where $T$ is the target temperature (K) for a specific trial, and $V_\text{gas}$ is the gas volume inside the sealed flask ($V_\text{gas} = V_\text{total volume} - V_\text{solution}$).

\begin{center}
  $\ast$~$\ast$~$\ast$
\end{center}

\noindent As a result, the research question for this investigation is:

\begin{center}
  \textit{How does the temperature affect the rate constant for the \ce{KI}-catalyzed decomposition of aqueous hydrogen peroxide, determined from the rate of increase of gas pressure in a sealed flask?}
\end{center}

\begin{center}
  $\ast$~$\ast$~$\ast$
\end{center}

% \subsection{Justification}
% \label{sec:justification}
\indent \indent Given that the rate constant of the decomposition of aqueous \ce{H2O2} is expected change with temperature, increasing $T$ should increase the fraction of particles reacting with enough energy to overcome the $E_{A}$, so that a greater proportion of particles can collide, leading to a reaction. Though \ce{KI} acts as a catalyst (provides an alternative pathway that has a lower $E_{A}$), the elementary steps still needs to overcome an $E_{A}$. Thus, the rate constant $k$ should increase with temperature, according to Arrhenius's equation (approximate linear relationship of $\ln{(k)}$ vs. $1/T$).

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:research-design}
\indent \indent This experiment was designed to isolate the temperature of the water bath as the sole independent variable affecting the pressure for the \ce{KI}-catalyzed decomposition reaction of aqueous \ce{H2O2}. Oxygen gas produced during the reaction increased the pressure inside a sealed $250\ \text{mL}$ flask. Thus, the increase in the rate of the initial pressure value was used to determine the rate constant at each temperature. A water bath was used to equilibrate the reaction system to within $1 ^\circ \text{C}$ of the target temperature. The masses of \ce{KI} were pre-measured and recorded before data collection, and sealed with plastic wrap and rubber band to prevent exposure to moisture.

\

% \subsection{Variables}
% \label{sec:research-design-variables}
\noindent \underline{\textbf{Independent variable:}} Temperature of the reaction system. The \ce{H2O2} in the sealed flask was equilibrated to the target temperature using a water bath before the start of each trial. The temperature was monitored using a Vernier Temperature Probe; trials were only started once the water bath temperature reached within $1\ ^\circ\text{C}$ of the target temperature.

\

\noindent \underline{\textbf{Dependent variable:}} Gas pressure in the sealed flask (kPa).

\

\noindent \underline{\textbf{Controlled variable:}} Initial amount of reactants, total volume of gas space in the sealed flask, environmental conditions, sampling settings, and apparatus setup were all kept constant. Variables were kept constant to reduce variations in the  rate.

\subsection{Procedure}
\label{sec:research-design-procedure}

\subsubsection{Part I: Setup}
\label{sec:research-design-procedure-setup}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Obtain safety goggles and lab coat.
  \item Gather the required items from \textit{section \ref{sec:research-design-materials}} to work station.
  \item Connect the gas pressure sensor to the LabQuest, and attach the plastic tubing to the pressure sensor.
  \item Connect the other end to the rubber stopper, and close the valve on the stopper.
  \item Secure the $250\ \text{mL}$ flask on the ring stand using a ring clamp, so that it remains stable throughout data collection.
  \item Add $25\ \text{mL}$ of distilled water to the $250\ \text{mL}$ flask (to represent \ce{H2O2} during setup).
  \item Place the glass bowl on the hot plate and add water so that it submerges the $25\ \text{mL}$ water inside the flask.
  \item Draw a horizontal line on the neck of the reaction flask to standardize the insertion depth of the rubber stopper in every trial.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item This ensures that the headspace gas volume ($V_\text{gas}$) remain constant between trials.
    \item Before inserting the rubber stopper each time, add a few drops of water to the rubber stopper to improve the sealing.
  \end{itemize}
  \item Ensure the temperature probe is not touching the bottom of the glass bowl or the sides of the flask. Secure the temperature probe using a ring clamp (to standardize the placement between trials)
  \item Set the LabQuest to record at \textit{5 samples / second}, for a total of \textit{60 seconds} for each trial.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Part II: Preparation of materials}
\label{sec:research-design-procedure-preparation-of-materials}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Pour a large amount of aqueous \ce{H2O2} into a clean $600\ \text{mL}$ beaker to use as the master solution for all trials. Excess can be poured back laterusing a funnel.
  \item Prepare potassium iodide portions for each trial:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Using a digital scale, weigh $0.30\ \text{g}$ of solid \ce{KI} into individual plastic cups.
    \item Label each cup with its corresponding temperature level and trial number.
    \item Seal each cup using a piece of plastic film, and secure it with rubber band to reduce exposure to moisture from air.
  \end{enumerate}
  \item Fill another clean $600\ \text{mL}$ beaker with ice. This will be used to lower the temperature of the water bath.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Part III: Calibration}
\label{sec:research-design-procedure-Calibration}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Open the valve on the rubber stopper so that the pressure sensor is open to the atmosphere. Record the atmospheric pressure for $10\ \text{seconds}$ and record the average pressure ($\text{kPa}$).
  \item Wash the $25\ \text{mL}$ pipette with aqueous \ce{H2O2} to avoid contamination, then dispose into the waste beaker.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent \underline{\textbf{Note:}}  An airtight-sealing calibration was not conducted because the experiment relies on finding the change in pressure over time. The pressure readings would be consistently lower than the actual pressure in the case of a minor leak, which does not affect the rate of change in pressure.

\subsubsection{Part IV: Experiment}
\label{sec:research-design-procedure-experiment}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Adjust the water bath temperature to the target temperature.
  \item Pipette $25\ \text{mL}$ of \ce{H2O2} to a clean $250\ \text{mL}$ beaker. Once the temperature of the water bath reaches within $1\ ^\circ\text{C}$ of the target temperature, pour the \ce{H2O2} into the reaction flask. Keep the reaction flask in the water bath to maintain consistency.
  \item Measure $5\ \text{mL}$ of distilled water using a $10\ \text{mL}$ graduated cylinder, and pour into the plastic cup containing the pre-weighed \ce{KI} for the trial. Stir to dissolve the \ce{KI}. Repeat twice, so that there is a total of $10\ \text{mL}$ of \ce{KI} solution.
  \item Start the data collection and collect a $3\ \text{second}$ baseline with the flask sealed. Then, remove the rubber stopper, and quickly add the \ce{KI} solution into the reaction flask, and immediately seal the flask.
  \item Swirl the flask gently for \textit{5\ \text{seconds}}, then keep the flask still for the remainder of the run.
  \item After data collection ends, save the run, and record the LabQuest run number with the corresponding trial.
  \item Dispose the solution into the waste beaker, and rinse the reaction flask once using tap water, then rinse again with distilled water.
  \item Repeat \textit{steps 1-7} for each trial at each temperature level, for a total of \textit{40 trials}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Part V: Cleanup}
\label{sec:research-design-procedure-cleanup}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Disessemble the apparatus.
  \item Save the file on the LabQuest, and download the data to a computer.
  \item Neutralize the waste solution by adding a small amount of sodium thiosulfate, then dispose the waste solution down the drain with running water. Return all equipments.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Sources of Uncertainty}
\label{sec:data-collection-sources-of-uncertainty}
\indent \indent To reduce random errors, 5 trials were conducted at each temperature level and averaged. Other variables, such as initial \ce{H2O2} concentration and volume, mass of \ce{KI}, apparatus setup, and sealing method were all kept constant across all runs so that differences in the initial pressure-increase rate can be attributed primary to temperature.

\subsection{Uncertainty Propagation}
\label{sec:data-processing-uncertainty-propagation}
\indent \indent The uncertainty values for temperature and pressure were obtained from official documentations of the equipments, and the smallest scale on the digital balance was used as the uncertainty of mass: $\pm 0.01\ \text{g}$. A $25\ \text{mL}$ volunmetric pipette was used to obtain the \ce{H2O2} for all trials, but the exact volumes delivered were not confirmed with a graduated cylinder. Thus, the pipette's tolerance was used as the volume uncertainty: $\pm 0.06\ \text{mL}$. Time was recorded automatically by the LabQuest, so its uncertainty was assumed negligible relative to pressure variation throughout the experiment.

However, the methodology in which the data were collected likely increased the uncertainties; for example, temperature varied during the $60\ \text{s}$ data collection period, and the probe may not have reflected the solution temperature. Moreover, though marked, the manual insertion depth of the stopper could have introduced small headspace variations. Thus, while linear interpolation with $\Delta{T}(x) = 0.20 + \frac{0.50 - 0.20}{100} \cdot x$ could have been used as the temperature probe's uncertainty, a conservative $\pm 0.5\ ^\circ$C was used for all temperature measurements.

\subsubsection{Uncertainty in oxygen production rate}
\indent \indent For each calculated rate, there contains random uncertainties and uncertainties from the instruments. Random uncertainty was propagated using the spread of the five trials' rates at each temperature level using standard deviation, and converted to uncertainty in the mean rate using the standard error of the mean ($\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{5}}$):

\begin{equation}
  \Delta{\overline{r}_\text{rand}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}
\end{equation}

\noindent Where $\sigma$ is the standard deviation of the trials. Instrument uncertainty was propagated through the rate calculation:

\begin{equation}
  \Delta{\overline{r}_\text{rel, inst}} = \sqrt{(\frac{\Delta{V}_\text{gas}}{V_\text{gas}})^2 + (\frac{\Delta{\overline{m}}}{\overline{m}})^2 + (\frac{\Delta{T}}{T})^2} 
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  \Delta{\overline{r}_\text{abs, inst}} = \overline{r} \cdot \Delta{\overline{r}_\text{rel, inst}}
\end{equation}

\noindent The uncertainty of $m$ was taken from the linear regression of the pressure-time data, and the uncertainty of $V_\text{gas}$ and $T$ were taken from \textit{table \ref{tab:data-processing-uncertainty-propagation-summary-of-uncertainties-by-tool}}. Combined:

\begin{equation}
  \Delta{\overline{r}} = \sqrt{(\Delta{\overline{r}_\text{rand}})^2 + (\Delta{\overline{r}_\text{abs, inst}})^2}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Example Calculation}
\label{sec:data-processing-example-calculation}
\indent \indent Consider $15\ ^\circ$C trial 1 as an example (marked as \textcolor{red}{red}) in \textit{table \ref{tab:processed-data}}. The average temperature for this trial will be used to determine $\Delta{T}$:

\begin{equation}
  T = 14.75 ^\circ \text{C} + 273.15 = 287.9 \text{K}
\end{equation}

\noindent The headspace gas volume was calculated as the difference between the flask volume and the solution volume:

\begin{equation}
  V_\text{gas} = 250\ \text{mL} - 25\ \text{mL} - 10\ \text{mL} = 215\ \text{mL} \rightarrow 2.15 \cdot 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^3
\end{equation}

\noindent The analysis window for the pressure-time graph was selected as the main region where pressure increased (from the start of sustained pressure increase to the end of the rise). For this trial, the selected interval was $37.8\ \text{s}$ to $60\ \text{s}$:

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=12cm]{Resources/example_calculation_data_helper.png}
	\caption{Pressure-time graph.}
	\label{fig:data-processing-example-calculation-pressure-time-graph}
\end{figure}

This window was chosen because the pressure before $t_\text{start} = 37.8\ \text{s}$ showed negligible change in pressure, while the pressure after $t_\text{start}$ increased consistently, due to \ce{O2_{(g)}} production. A linear regression with $y = mx + b$ was then fitted to the 111 datapoints in the selected window:

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Resources/example_calculation_time_snapshot.png}
	\caption{Selected analysis window.}
	\label{fig:data-processing-example-calculation-selected-analysis-window}
\end{figure}

\noindent Note that the slope $m$ was recorded as $0.01486\ \text{kPa}\ \text{s}^{-1}$, which converts to $14.86\ \text{Pa}\ \text{s}^{-1}$. The rate of \ce{O2} production was then calculated from the ideal gas relationship. For a constant headspace volume:

\begin{equation}
  r = \frac{\Delta{n(\ce{O2})}}{\Delta{t}} = \frac{V_\text{gas}}{RT} \cdot (\frac{\Delta{P}}{\Delta{t}}) = \frac{V_\text{gas}}{RT} \cdot m
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  r = \frac{2.15 \cdot 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^3}{(8.31\ \text{J}\ \text{K}\ \text{mol}^{-1})(288.15\ \text{K})} \cdot 14.86\ \text{Pa}\ \text{s}^{-1} 
\end{equation}

\begin{center}
  $r = 1.33 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{mol}\ \text{s}^{-1}$
\end{center}

\noindent The process above was repeated to calculated the average rate for the other 4 trials at $15\ ^\circ$C:

\begin{equation}
  \overline{r} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n=5} r_i
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  \overline{r} = \frac{(1.33 \cdot 10^{-6}) + (1.85 \cdot 10^{-6}) + (2.30 \cdot 10^{-6}) + (1.86 \cdot 10^{-6})}{4} = 1.82 \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{mol}\ \text{s}^{-1}
\end{equation}

\noindent \underline{\textbf{Note:}} Trial 5 was not included in the calculations and justified in \textit{section \ref{sec:data-processing}}.

\

\noindent The random uncertainty can then be calculated:

\begin{equation}
  \sigma = \sqrt{\frac{1}{4}(-0.49 \cdot 10^{-6})^2 + (0.03 \cdot 10^{-6})^2 + (0.48 \cdot 10^{-6})^2 + (0.04 \cdot 10^{-6})^2}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation*}
  \sigma = 3.52 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{mol}\ \text{s}^{-1}
\end{equation*}

\begin{equation}
  \Delta{\overline{r}_\text{rand}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{4}} = 1.65 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{mol}\ \text{s}^{-1}
\end{equation}

\noindent \textbf{Calculating instrumental uncertainty from known variables:}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Average temperature range in this level: $0.4\ ^\circ \text{C} \rightarrow $ half-range = $0.2\ ^\circ$C
  \begin{equation*}
    \Delta{T} = \max{(0.5, 0.2)} = \pm 0.5\ \text{K}
  \end{equation*}
  \begin{equation}
    \therefore \frac{\Delta{T}}{T} = \frac{0.5\ \text{K}}{288.15\ \text{K}} = 0.001735 \rightarrow 0.174\%
  \end{equation}
  \item Average slope uncertainty (from Microsoft Excel's Regression tool): $\pm 0.457\ \text{Pa}\ \text{s}^{-1}$
  \begin{equation}
    \frac{\Delta{m}}{m} = \frac{0.457\ \text{Pa}}{14.86\ \text{Pa}} = 0.0307 \rightarrow 3.07\%
  \end{equation}
  \item Volume uncertainty: $\pm 0.06\ \text{mL}$:
  \begin{equation}
    \frac{\Delta{V_\text{gas}}}{V_\text{gas}} = \frac{0.06\ \text{mL}}{215\ \text{mL}} = 0.000279 \rightarrow 0.0279\%
  \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The instrumental uncertainties are then combined:

\begin{equation}
  \Delta{\overline{r}_\text{rel, inst}} = \sqrt{(0.001735)^2 + (0.0307)^2 + (0.000279)^2} = 0.03075 \rightarrow 3.08\%
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  \therefore \Delta{\overline{r}_\text{abs, inst}} = \overline{r} \cdot \Delta{\overline{r}_\text{rel, inst}} = (1.82 \cdot 10^{-6}) \cdot 0.03075 = 5.60 \cdot 10^{-8}\ \text{mol}\ \text{s}^{-1}
\end{equation}

\noindent Final uncertainty for the average rate since they are independent uncertainties:

\begin{equation}
  \Delta{\overline{r}} = \sqrt{(1.65 \cdot 10^{-7})^2 + (5.60 \cdot 10^{-8})^2}
\end{equation}

\begin{center}
  $\Delta{\overline{r}} = 1.74 \cdot 10^{-7}\ \text{mol}\ \text{s}^{-1}$
\end{center}

\section{Data Analysis}
\label{sec:data-analysis}
\indent \indent Data from \textit{table \ref{tab:processed-data}} and \textit{table \ref{tab:data-processing-example-calculation-oxygen-production-rate}} were used to create an average initial rate ($\text{mol}\ \text{s}^{-1}$) vs. temperature ($\text{K}$) graph:

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Resources/data_analysis_main_graph.png}
	\caption{Graph of average rate ($\text{mol}\ \text{s}^{-1}$) vs. temperature level ($\text{K}$).}
	\label{fig:data-analysis-main-graph}
\end{figure}

\noindent As shown above, there is an increasing exponential relationship between the rate of \ce{O2} production and the temperature of the system:

\begin{equation}
  f(x) = 0.0005e^{0.0768x}
\end{equation}

This supports the collision theory outlined in \textit{section \ref{sec:introduction}}: higher temperature increases the frequency of collisions and the fraction of molecules with sufficient energy to overcome the activation energy; therefore, the rate of successful reactions are increased.

Given that \ce{[H2O2]} and the mass of \ce{KI} were held constant (minor variations in the mass of \ce{KI}), the measured oxygen production rate is proportional to the rate constant by a constant scale factor difference:

\begin{equation}
  r = C \cdot k
\end{equation}

\noindent Where $C$ is the scaling factor between the oxygen production rate and the true rate constant, only when initial reactants are constant. This allows $r$ to replace $k$ in the Arrhenius equation:

\begin{equation}
  \ln{(r)} = \ln{(C \cdot k)} = \ln{(C)} + \ln{(k)}
\end{equation}

\noindent Thus, plotting $\ln{(r)}$ against $\frac{1}{T}$ will still yields a straight line with slope $-\frac{E_\text{A}}{R}$, only shifting the intercept by $\ln{(C)}$:

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Resources/data_analysis_arrhenius_plot.png}
	\caption{Graph of $\ln{(\overline{r})}$ vs. $\frac{1}{T}$.}
	\label{fig:data-analysis-arrhenius-plot}
\end{figure}

The linear fit above produced a slope of $-6360\ \text{K}$ and $R^2 = 0.9438$, which indicates a strong linear relationship between $\ln{(r)}$ and $\frac{1}{T}$.

Recall from \textit{equation \ref{eq:arrhenius-equation}} that the slope of $\ln{(k)}$ vs. $\frac{1}{T}$ is $-\frac{E_\text{A}}{R}$. Given that $r \propto k$ in this experiment, the slope is used to find the apparent activation energy:

\begin{equation}
  E_\text{A} = |-m \cdot R| = 6360\ \text{K} \cdot 8.31\ \text{J}\ \text{K}^{-1}\ \text{mol}^{-1} = 52851.6\ \text{J}\ \text{mol}^{-1} \rightarrow 52.9\ \text{kJ}\ \text{mol}^{-1}
\end{equation}

\begin{center}
  $\therefore E_\text{A} = 52.9 \pm 0.6\ \text{kJ}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$ \footnote{Uncertainty obtained using Microsoft Excel's LINEST function.}
\end{center}

Although the overall fit is strong, there are small deviations from the trendline relative to other points that occur at, for example, the fifth datapoint ($35\ ^\circ \text{C}$). A possible explanation for this is the silghtly higher average mass of \ce{KI} at that level specifically. The average mass was $0.31\ \text{g}$ compared to an average of $0.29$ or $0.30\ \text{g}$ at other temperature levels. A slightly larger \ce{KI} mass increases the amount of \ce{I-}, which can increase the \ce{O2} production rate and $\ln{(\overline{r})}$ for that level.

At the same time, the mean \ce{O2} production rate increased from $\overline{r} = (1.89 \pm 0.18) \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{mol}\ \text{s}^{-1}$ at $288.15\ \text{K}$ ($15\ ^\circ \text{C}$) to $\overline{r} = (1.84 \pm 0.38) \cdot 10^{-5}\ \text{mol}\ \text{s}^{-1}$ at $323.15\ \text{K}$ ($50\ ^\circ \text{C}$) (\textit{table \ref{tab:data-processing-example-calculation-oxygen-production-rate}}), which corresponds to an increase by a factor of $x$:

\begin{multicols}{2}
  \noindent \textbf{Calculation:}
  \begin{equation*}
    x = \frac{\overline{r}_\text{323.15\ \text{K}}}{\overline{r}_\text{288.15\ \text{K}}} = \frac{1.84 \cdot 10^{-5}}{1.89 \cdot 10^{-6}}
  \end{equation*}

  \vspace{-\baselineskip}
  \begin{equation*}
    x = 9.74
  \end{equation*}

  \columnbreak

  \noindent \textbf{Uncertainty:}
  \begin{equation*}
    \frac{\Delta{F}}{F} = \frac{\Delta{\overline{r}_\text{323.15\ \text{K}}}}{\overline{r}_\text{323.15\ \text{K}}} + \frac{\Delta{\overline{r}_\text{288.15\ \text{K}}}}{\overline{r}_\text{288.15\ \text{K}}}
  \end{equation*}

  \vspace{-\baselineskip}
  \begin{equation*}
    \frac{\Delta{F}}{F} = \frac{0.38}{1.84} + \frac{0.18}{1.89} = 0.302
  \end{equation*}

  \vspace{-\baselineskip}
  \begin{equation*}
    \Delta{F} = 0.302 \cdot 9.74 = 2.94
  \end{equation*}
\end{multicols}

\begin{center}
  $\Downarrow$
\end{center}

\vspace{-\baselineskip}
\begin{equation}
  \therefore x = 9.74 \pm 2.94
\end{equation}

This increase by almost 10 times supports the research question, that higher temperature increases the rate constant.

Because of the deviation in the fifth datapoint (explained earlier), a graph of the mass of \ce{KI} vs. rate per trial was plotted to check whether small variations in the mass of \ce{KI} had an effect on the reaction rate. However, no clear relationship was observed, which shows that the small variations in the mass of \ce{KI} was not the primary cause of the observed temperature dependence of $\overline{r}$, and that the higher average \ce{KI} mass in the $35\ ^\circ \text{C}$ was only regional to that level.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
\indent \indent This investigation aims to examine the relationship between reaction temperature and the rate constant for the \ce{KI}-catalyzed decomposition reaction of \ce{H2O2}, determined from the rate of \ce{O2} pressure increase in a sealed flask. The processed data confirms that increasing temperature increases the apparent rate constant, consistent with the Arrhenius model and collision theory. Across the experimental range of the data, the mean oxygen production rate increased from $\overline{r} = (1.89 \pm 0.18) \cdot 10^{-6}\ \text{mol}\ \text{s}^{-1}$ at $288.15\ \text{K}$ ($15\ ^\circ \text{C}$) to $\overline{r} = (1.84 \pm 0.38) \cdot 10^{-5}\ \text{mol}\ \text{s}^{-1}$ at $323.15\ \text{K}$ ($50\ ^\circ \text{C}$), corresponding to a $9.74 \pm 2.94$ increase in rate. The Arrhenius plot yielded an activation energy of $E_\text{A} = 52.9 \pm 0.6\ \text{kJ}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$ for the catalyzed pathway. Compared to accepted scientific values, the uncatalyzed decomposition of \ce{H2O2} has an $E_\text{A}$ of approximately $75\ \text{kJ}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$, while the catalyzed pathway lowers it to approximately $56$ to $56.5\ \text{kJ}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$. However, the experimental value of $52.9 \pm 0.6\ \text{kJ}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$ falls $3$ to $3.5\ \text{kJ}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$ below the accepted catalyzed range, which could be attributed to the systematic underestimation of the initial rate at higher temperatures: the reaction occurs at a faster rate at higher temperature levels; thus, some \ce{O2_{(g)}} could have escaped before the flask was fully sealed. This reduces the pressure-time slope, which compresses the range of $\ln{(\overline{r})}$ values and flattens the slope of the Arrhenius plot, leading to a lower $E_\text{A}$.

Overall, this investigation confirms that increasing temperature does increase the measured rate constant for the \ce{KI}-catalyzed decomposition of \ce{H2O2}; the exponential relationship between temperature and rate, the almost tenfold increase in rate over a $35\ ^\circ \text{C}$ range in reaction temperature, and the linear relationship depicted in the Arrhenius plot are all consistent with the collision theory and the Arrhenius model, that \ce{KI} provides an alternative reaction pathway that requires less energy.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}


\

\hrule

\

\indent \indent A key strength was controlling variables.The same apparatus (flask, stopper, tubing, pressure sensor) was used throughout, and the initial reactant amounts were held constant, so changes in the reaction rate are primarily due to temperature. Minor fluctuations in the mass of \ce{KI} were present, but as \textit{figure \ref{fig:data-analysis-ki-mass}} has proved, they are not the dominant influence on $k_{app}$.

Another strength was replication. Learning from past mistakes in other IB Science investigations, conducting five trials per temperature level allowed mean values to be used, reducing random error and making the trend in $k_{app}$ vs. temperature less dependent on a single trial. It also improved stability when a trial and using mean values reduced the impact of random variations between trials and increased stability when a trial needs to be excluded (i.e. due to missing source data or other experimental errors).

% \indent \indent A further strength is the use of continuous sensor-based data collection to determine $k_{\text{app}}$ from a defined early-time window. Because the pressure sensor produced many datapoints over the measurement interval, the calculated slopes were less dependent on individual noisy readings and could better represent a consistent rate proxy than a simple two-point calculation.
\indent \indent A major methodological limitation was maitaining a stable and uniform water bath temperature during the $60\ \text{seconds}$ collection period. While most trials started and ended within $1 ^\circ \text{C}$ of the target temperature, gradual drift could occur amidst the collection period due to heat loss to surroundings. Moreover, from personal experience, the temperature probe may not have perfectly represented the temperature of the entire water bath; readings often drifted when blocks of ice drifted nearby. To reduce this limitation, a laboratory thermostatic water bath with internal circulation would provide a more uniform temperature control. However, the high cost of such equipment made it impractical for this investigation.

A second limitation was the reaction temperature inside the flask. The solution inside the flask may not have fully equilibrated with the bath during the fitted interval due to the addition of the catalyst (prepared by dissolving solid \ce{KI} in water at room-temperature), which would increase the spread of data in $k_{app}$ and reduce accuracy of teh temperature assigned to each trial. This can be improved by pre-equilibrating the \ce{KI} solution in the same bath, and measure the temperature inside the flask using a 3-hole stopper with a sealed port for the temperature probe.

Lastly, adding the catalyst required breifly opening the system. At higher temperatures, the reaction could begin before a complete seal was achieved, which lead to an underestimation of the initial rate. Injecting the catalyst using a syringe would allow for a more consistent start time across trials and a more proper seal on the rubber stopper.

By implementing these improvements in future iterations, a more complete characterization of how the reaction temperature influences the inital reaction rate of the decomposition reaction of hydrogen peroxide.
